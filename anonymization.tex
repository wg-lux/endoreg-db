\title{Device-Aware Endoscopic Video De-identification with Temporal Sensitive Metadata Accumulation}
\author{Thomas J. Lux, Max Hild, SM Hamza Zahid}
\date{February 2026}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Endoscopic video and associated reports routinely contain direct and indirect
identifiers, including patient demographics, operator names, examination
timestamps, accession identifiers, and institutional metadata. These elements
are clinically useful during care, but they complicate secondary use for quality
improvement, education, and medical artificial intelligence. GDPR Article 17
codifies a right to erasure and motivates technical controls that connect
de-identification, storage, validation, and deletion across the media lifecycle.
\cite{gdpr} This paper describes a methods-oriented workflow for endoscopy
environments that combines temporal sensitive metadata accumulation,
processor-aware video anonymization, protected storage boundaries, and governed
human validation. The workflow treats deterministic hashes as controlled linkage
identifiers rather than as evidence of anonymity. Its purpose is not to claim
fully automated anonymization, but to provide an auditable process for reducing
privacy risk while preserving clinically useful video content.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern endoscopy systems superimpose textual and symbolic information directly
onto the live image stream. The overlay may contain patient name elements, date
of birth, accession or case identifiers, examination date and time, examiner
identity, device status, and center labels. In the clinical setting, these data
support continuity of care and medico-legal traceability. In secondary-use
settings, however, they can remain visible as burnt-in protected health
information (PHI) even when database fields have been removed or transformed.

The management and sharing of healthcare data for research purposes present
substantial challenges in security, privacy, and regulatory compliance,
particularly under frameworks such as HIPAA and GDPR.
\cite{barbaria2025advancing}\cite{prakaashana2025measuring} These challenges
are intensified in endoscopic video because identifiers may be embedded in
pixels rather than stored as separable metadata. Recent work on burnt-in text
recognition in medical imaging has emphasized the practical difficulty of
detecting text under compression artifacts, motion blur, low resolution, and
background interference. \cite{osagie2024burnt}

The clinical problem is therefore not simply to remove visible text. A useful
de-identification workflow must reduce identifying context while preserving
mucosal findings, instrument position, procedure dynamics, and reviewable
provenance. This paper presents such a workflow as an implementation-oriented
method. The central argument is that endoscopy de-identification should be
treated as a governed process in which temporal evidence, device-specific
geometry, protected storage, and human validation are connected explicitly.

\section{Related Work}
Research on medical data sharing has long recognized the tension between
privacy protection and the need for sufficiently large, diverse, and annotated
datasets. Foundation-model and medical AI literature has emphasized that
generalization depends on data volume, representativeness, and annotation
quality. \cite{moor2023foundation} Synthetic and simulated records can support
some development workflows, but freely distributable clinical records remain
limited. \cite{walonoski2018synthea} In surgical and endoscopic video, dataset
creation additionally requires careful de-identification because privacy risks
may be present in both metadata and image pixels. \cite{bilal2025temset}

Existing approaches to burned-in text have often relied on broad suppression of
text-bearing regions or on heuristic rules for particular PHI formats. Such
methods can reduce immediate disclosure risk, but they may discard clinically
useful visual information or fail when devices, firmware, or local recording
styles change. \cite{macdonald2024method} Recent evaluations of
vision-language systems also caution that spatial masking does not necessarily
eliminate all privacy risk when contextual identifiers remain inferable.
\cite{young2025vision} These observations motivate a workflow that combines
text detection, temporal aggregation, device-aware region handling, and
post-processing validation rather than relying on a single image-processing
operation.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{System Context and Data Flow}
The proposed workflow is designed for clinical endoscopy environments in which
videos and reports originate from heterogeneous rooms, processors, codecs, and
capture pipelines. The canonical media object is stored in protected managed
storage. External tools that require a filesystem path receive a local plaintext
working copy for the duration of processing, and streamable artifacts are
generated only when protected media delivery policy permits them.

\subsection{Temporal Sensitive Metadata Accumulation}
Sensitive metadata accumulation integrates observations from sampled frames into
a patient- and procedure-level representation. Single-frame OCR is often
insufficient because overlays may be transient, blurred, partially occluded, or
rendered with changing contrast. A temporal model allows partial detections to
be merged over time, supports preservation of high-confidence fields, and makes
uncertainty visible for validation rather than treating absence of detection as
evidence of safety.

During import and the first anonymization pass, the video workflow uses
sampled-frame OCR and metadata extraction. The current video path preferentially
uses TesserOCR when available and falls back to PyTesseract for compatibility.
\cite{tesserocr2018}\cite{smith2007overview} Broader image and report
processing can combine text detection and OCR components such as EAST,
Tesseract-based detection, and additional OCR engines where configured.
\cite{ZERONE2024}\cite{Ribeiro2019OCRcompare} These components are best
understood as detection instruments rather than as safety guarantees; their
clinical adequacy depends on local recall requirements and validation policy.

\subsection{Device-Aware Video Anonymization}
Endoscopy platforms differ in overlay placement, viewport geometry, metadata
panel structure, font rendering, and firmware-dependent layout. A single fixed
mask is therefore rarely appropriate across device families. The workflow uses
processor-specific region-of-interest (ROI) definitions to separate the
endoscopic viewport from regions in which identifying overlays are expected.
The anonymization step can then preserve diagnostically relevant image content
while suppressing surrounding panels, banners, or detected PHI regions.

Three strategy families are relevant in this setting. Mask-based viewport
preservation suppresses expected metadata regions according to processor
geometry. Box-level or word-level masking uses PHI region proposals when
detection output is sufficiently localized. Frame-level suppression or removal
is reserved for frames or segments judged to contain high-risk identifiers or
material unsuitable for downstream clinical analysis. Selection among these
strategies should be determined by local release policy, research protocol, and
the intended clinical use of the resulting dataset.

\subsection{Pseudonymized Linkage}
The workflow uses deterministic linkage identifiers to group related records,
examinations, and report/video pairs without exposing direct identifiers in the
secondary-use dataset. This is a governance and linkage mechanism, not an
anonymization guarantee. If the input attributes, salt, keying material, or
linkage context are known, deterministic hashes can remain personal data under
GDPR. The manuscript therefore treats such values as controlled pseudonyms that
require access control, audit, rotation or replacement policy, and separation
from direct identifiers.

\section{Governance and Validation}
\subsection{Human Review and Release Control}
The validation interface supports direct comparison of raw and processed media
for authorized reviewers. Video review can present raw and anonymized streams
side by side, synchronized during playback, and in context with segment
annotations. Report review can similarly compare raw and anonymized text or PDF
representations. This review step is essential because automated OCR and
masking are probabilistic and because residual privacy risk may arise from
context, not only from recognized text.

For video, the ``outside'' label denotes segments acquired outside the
patient's body or otherwise unsuitable for downstream clinical analysis. Such
segments may be reviewed, removed, censored, or excluded from derived datasets
according to local policy. Release should depend on visual review, metadata
validation, outside-segment handling, and an auditable record of the decision.

\subsection{Storage and Streaming Boundary}
The storage model separates canonical protected media from temporary processing
copies and optional streamable artifacts. Canonical files remain in protected
managed storage. Plaintext working copies are materialized only for tools that
require filesystem paths and should be cleaned after processing. Optional
streamable artifacts are permitted only on protected media delivery paths and
must not expose encrypted storage bytes directly to clients.

\subsection{Deletion and the Right to Erasure}
Safe deletion in endoscopy anonymization is a controlled clinical workflow
rather than a single database action. It should combine transaction boundaries,
storage-aware file removal, scoped annotation cleanup, and durable operation
logs. The right to erasure should not be conflated with pseudonymization: a
hash may preserve linkage after direct identifiers have been removed and may
therefore remain relevant to privacy governance.

Destructive operations should be idempotent and scoped. Metadata updates,
annotation cleanup, and parent-record state transitions should either complete
together or fail in a way that preserves reviewable logs. Hard deletion should
be gated by role, retention, and governance policy. Post-deletion verification
should confirm that orphaned annotations, plaintext temporary files, and
inconsistent state records have not been left behind.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
The current manuscript should be read as a methods paper with planned
evaluation and pilot evidence. Because the right to erasure may prevent
long-term preservation of identifiable training material, PHI detection and
evaluation should occur on premise during import or in a similarly governed
local environment. Quantitative claims should be added only after local pilot
data are available.

\section{Limitations}
The workflow is intentionally conservative in its claims. Sparse sampling may
miss transient identifiers. OCR uncertainty can propagate into incomplete
metadata accumulation, particularly in low-quality captures. Device firmware
updates or local configuration changes may invalidate ROI assumptions. Box- or
word-level masking can preserve more clinical context than broad masking, but
only when PHI region detection is sufficiently reliable. Human validation
therefore remains part of the method rather than an optional afterthought.

The legal status of processed media is also context-dependent. Deterministic
pseudonyms facilitate longitudinal linkage and audit, but they do not by
themselves establish anonymity. Similarly, protected streamable artifacts may be
operationally necessary for review, yet they require access control and
deployment safeguards. The evaluation should therefore report technical
performance together with governance conditions under which release is allowed.

\section{Conclusion}
Endoscopic video de-identification is best understood as a clinical governance
workflow supported by technical controls, not as a one-time image transform.
Temporal sensitive metadata accumulation addresses the incompleteness of
single-frame OCR, device-aware anonymization preserves more diagnostic content
than indiscriminate suppression, and protected storage boundaries reduce the
risk of unmanaged plaintext media. Deterministic hashes can support linkage and
audit, but they remain pseudonymization mechanisms and must not be presented as
proof of anonymity. The proposed workflow therefore emphasizes auditable
de-identification, human validation, and cautious evaluation before secondary
use of endoscopic video and report data.
